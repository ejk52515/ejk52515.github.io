// bio

The long way around was the point.

Helpdesk, then finance, then back to infrastructure — each stop added something the next one needed.

I got my start where a lot of good sysadmins do: on the Towson University helpdesk, four years of tickets, walk-ups, and escalations across a mixed Windows and macOS environment. That's where the troubleshooting instinct formed — isolate the variable, check the obvious thing first, write down what actually worked.

Then I took a detour that turned out not to be one. As an investment analyst at Cambridge Associates, I supported the management of over $1B in assets across 13 endowments and foundations — building SQL and Power Query reporting workflows and conducting due diligence in an industry where controls aren't bureaucracy, they're the product. I learned why regulated environments operate the way they do from the inside. When I talk to compliance or risk teams now, we speak the same language.

The pull back to technology won, and I've spent the years since as a Systems Administrator at Jefe Consulting — running Active Directory for 400+ users, Group Policy across 300+ workstations, hybrid M365/Entra ID with Conditional Access and phased MFA rollout, Nessus vulnerability management, Splunk security monitoring, and ServiceNow automation.

Somewhere along the way the job changed shape for me: I stopped thinking of infrastructure as things you configure and started treating it as things you declare. That's why my recent work — a domain controller deployed and promoted entirely through Terraform, a policy-enforced patching pipeline — is built in code rather than by hand. If it isn't in code and documentation, it doesn't really exist.

Right now I'm working through the AZ-104, with AWS Solutions Architect and the Red Hat track (RHCSA → RHCE) queued behind it, running a homelabs that never sits idle. The direction is clear: cloud infrastructure with a security spine.
